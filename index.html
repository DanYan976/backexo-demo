Adaptive, simulation‑trained control for low‑back exoskeletons

Back-support exoskeletons work less well in real workplaces, because their control gives every worker the same help. We train one adaptive controller in simulation, across many body sizes, loads and lifts, so it adjusts its assist to each worker. Then we run it on a commercial active exoskeleton.

The problem

Strains and sprains are the biggest injury category. Musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) come from repetitive lifting, awkward postures and heavy loads. Public safety reports from one of the largest warehouse operators show how big the problem still is.

The safety limit

We judge every lift against the NIOSH spine limit. It is the biomechanical basis of the Revised NIOSH Lifting Equation, a common tool for rating how risky a lift is.

3.4 kN

L5/S1 disc compression that NIOSH uses to define increased low-back injury risk.

Waters et al., Ergonomics 1993

We measure it

Our models estimate the load at L5/S1 for every lift, with and without the exo.

Why exos struggle

Exos help in the lab, but struggle at work. Lab tests show less back-muscle effort. In real workplaces, three problems get in the way.

In the lab

~20%

less back-muscle activity during lifting, on average.

25% with active exos, 18% with passive. A 2026 meta-analysis of 50 studies (787 people) confirms a modest but significant drop with passive exos. Most testers were young men (~78%).

Kermavnar et al., Ergonomics 2021 (33 studies); Favro et al., Applied Ergonomics 2026 (50 studies); Kazemi et al., Applied Ergonomics 2026 (191 studies)

At work

Sudden torque spikes

Rule-based control can jump to a large torque at once. Uncomfortable, even unsafe.

Same help for everyone

The assist does not change with the worker, the load or the lifting style.

Benefit is hard to prove

Spine load is rarely measured on the job, so injury prevention is hard to show.

The problem is control, not hardware

Passive springs

Same support for every lift. Resists walking and sitting, and cannot react to load.

Hand-tuned rules

Simple rules (e.g., more help the more you bend), set on a few lab subjects. Ignore who lifts and how heavy.

Per-person tuning

Fits one person, but takes hours of testing per worker and per task. Too slow for a workforce.

What is needed: one controller that adapts to each worker's size, load and technique, needs no tuning sessions, gives smooth and stable torque, and cuts spine load.

Our approach

One adaptive controller, built in five steps. We build digital human models, teach them real motion and real lifts, train the exo controller on them in simulation, then run it on a real exoskeleton.

01

Digital human model

We use a full-body musculoskeletal model driven by 416 muscles. Fitted to each person's motion capture, it gives the load on every spinal joint. A model of the FIT HV couples onto it like the real device.

02

Learn from real motion

Lab recordings, even a plain video, become reference motions. The digital human learns to reproduce them in physics with its own muscles, so we see how hard each muscle works.

03

Lift boxes in simulation

The digital human lifts real boxes in physics, with the techniques workers use (full squat, semi squat and stoop), and carries them to a shelf.

04

Train the exo controller

The digital human wears the exo. One controller learns by trial and error, across thousands of worker, load and technique combinations, before anyone wears the device.

05

Deploy on hardware

Our controller drives the FIT HV motors in real time. We test in the lab first, then in real workplaces.

Publications

Published work on training exoskeleton controllers in simulation. In each paper, an exo controller is trained with reinforcement learning on musculoskeletal models, then tested on a real exoskeleton.

  1. NatureJun 2024

    Experiment-free exoskeleton assistance via learning in simulation

    S. Luo, M. Jiang, S. Zhang, J. Zhu, S. Yu, I. Dominguez Silva, T. Wang, E. Rouse, B. Zhou, H. Yuk, X. Zhou, H. Su

    A hip-exo controller learned entirely in simulation, with no human experiments in training. On a real hip exo, it cut energy use by 24.3% when walking, 13.1% when running and 15.4% when climbing stairs.

  2. arXivOct 2026

    Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning of Co-Adaptive Hip Exoskeleton Control (MARL-Exo) for Metabolic Energy Reduction

    Y. Yuan, J. Wolf, G. Androwis, X. Zhou

    A full-body muscle model with varied body sizes, gaits and speeds trains together with a hip-exo controller that is rewarded for lowering muscle energy use. The controller sees only the hip angles and its own past commands. Moved to a real hip exo with no retraining, it cut the energy cost of walking by 18.3% compared with the unpowered device, in all eight adults tested.

  3. arXivSep 2026

    UniExo: Unified Multi-Skill Policies for Musculoskeletal Locomotion and Co-Adaptive Exoskeleton Control

    Y. Yuan, J. Wolf, G. Androwis, X. Zhou

    One human model learns to walk, turn, run and walk backward, and one hip-exo controller trains with it, so the exo adapts to each skill with no labels or mode switching. On a real hip exo, it worked at four treadmill speeds with six people and guided one person through a route mixing all four skills.

  4. arXivAug 2026

    Staged Multi-Agent Training for Human-Exoskeleton Co-Adaptation: Simulation-Based Learning and Real-World Metabolic and Biomechanical Validation

    Y. Yuan, J. Wolf, G. Androwis, X. Zhou

    The human model and the hip-exo controller train together in simulation, in stages, then the controller moves to a real hip exo with no per-person retraining. In eight adults, powered assist cut energy use by 19.7% compared with the unpowered device. Extended version of the IROS 2026 paper below.

  5. IROS 2026arXiv, Mar 2026

    SMAT: Staged Multi-Agent Training for Co-Adaptive Exoskeleton Control

    Y. Yuan, G. Androwis, X. Zhou

    A four-stage training plan in which the human model and the exo controller learn step by step, then adapt to each other. In simulation, hip muscle activation fell 10.1%. On a real hip exo with five people on a treadmill, it gave mostly helpful (positive) power.
